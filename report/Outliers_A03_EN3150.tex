\section*{Executive Summary}
Deploying vision-based automated material sorting on autonomous recycling collection nodes requires balancing high classification accuracy against strict hardware limitations. Embedded microcontrollers and edge perception sensors often operate with under 512\,KB of SRAM, 1--2\,MB of flash memory, and battery-constrained thermal power envelopes. 

In this investigation, Team Outliers investigates the design, parameter optimization, arithmetic complexity, and hardware profiling of edge-oriented vision models on the 9-class RealWaste dataset downscaled to $64 \times 64$ resolution. We design a conventional 3-stage convolutional network (\textbf{Model A}, 102,313 parameters) and architect a highly compact depthwise-separable network (\textbf{Model B}, 22,297 parameters) engineered strictly beneath the 100,000 parameter budget. Furthermore, we benchmark these custom models against fine-tuned state-of-the-art mobile backbones (\textbf{MobileNetV2} and \textbf{ShuffleNetV2 1.0$\times$}). 

Our empirical results demonstrate that Model B achieves \textbf{69.85\% top-1 test accuracy}, surpassing Model A (67.32\%) while reducing parameter count by \textbf{78.2\%} and multiply-accumulate operations (MACs) by \textbf{85.0\%} (from 41.30M to 6.19M). Model B occupies only \textbf{0.10\,MB (98\,KB)} of serialized memory, making it uniquely suited for on-chip deployment on resource-constrained microcontrollers like the STM32H7 or ESP32-S3. Conversely, while MobileNetV2 and ShuffleNetV2 achieve higher classification accuracy (88.22\% and 88.64\%), their substantial flash footprints ($>4.9$\,MB) and memory traffic render them prohibitive for microcontrollers, establishing an empirical Pareto frontier for smart recycling edge infrastructure.

\section{Introduction and Edge Computing Objectives}
\label{sec:intro}
%==============================================================================

Automated municipal and industrial solid-waste sorting is a vital prerequisite for modern resource recovery and circular economy initiatives. Conventional sorting facilities rely on centralized sensor arrays coupled to remote server clusters. However, transmitting uncompressed high-resolution video streams across wireless links introduces latency bottlenecks, requires sustained network availability, and exposes municipal data streams to network congestion.

Edge intelligence provides an alternative paradigm: executing neural inference directly at the optical sensor node. In such setups, camera modules embedded into sorting chutes or autonomous collection bins categorize materials immediately upon image capture. The primary challenge stems from the operational envelope of edge silicon:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Strict Memory Boundaries:} Microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M4/M7, ESP32-S3) possess small on-chip static RAM (often 256\,KB to 512\,KB) and limited non-volatile NOR Flash (typically 1\,MB to 2\,MB). Large neural weights cannot be buffered or executed without external memory chips, which dramatically increases unit cost and power draw.
  \item \textbf{Arithmetic and Energy Budgets:} Low-power edge processors lack dedicated desktop GPUs. Every multiply-accumulate (MAC) operation consumes valuable clock cycles and battery energy.
  \item \textbf{Visual Ambiguity at Low Spatial Resolution:} To conform to buffer limits, edge frames must be downscaled (here, $64 \times 64$ pixels), which compresses fine textures and intensifies inter-class ambiguity between visually similar waste streams.
\end{itemize}

This investigation explores these trade-offs by evaluating custom convolutional architectures against modern mobile transfer learning backbones, providing empirical guidance for edge machine vision design.

\section{Architectural Design: Baseline vs. Depthwise Separable CNN}
\label{sec:architectures}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Mathematical Foundations of Lightweight Convolutions}
Standard 2D convolution applies a 4D kernel tensor $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{out} \times C_{in} \times K \times K}$ simultaneously across all spatial and channel dimensions. For an input feature map $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{in} \times H \times W}$, computing an output activation at spatial coordinate $(i, j)$ requires:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{Y}_{k, i, j} = \sum_{c=1}^{C_{in}} \sum_{m=-k_h}^{k_h} \sum_{n=-k_w}^{k_w} \mathbf{W}_{k, c, m, n} \, \mathbf{X}_{c, i+m, j+n} + \mathbf{b}_k
\end{equation}
The computational cost in MAC operations is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{MACs}_{\text{standard}} = H_{out} \times W_{out} \times C_{in} \times C_{out} \times K^2
\end{equation}

Depthwise Separable Convolutions (pioneered by Howard et al.~\cite{mobilenets}) factorize this operation into two disjoint phases:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Depthwise Spatial Convolution:} A single $K \times K$ filter is applied per input channel independently ($G = C_{in}$ groups):
  \begin{equation}
    \text{MACs}_{\text{depthwise}} = H_{out} \times W_{out} \times C_{in} \times K^2
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Pointwise Channel Projection:} A $1 \times 1$ linear convolution combines channel representations across the depth:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{MACs}_{\text{pointwise}} = H_{out} \times W_{out} \times C_{in} \times C_{out} \times 1^2
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

The ratio of arithmetic operations is given analytically by:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\text{MACs}_{\text{depthwise}} + \text{MACs}_{\text{pointwise}}}{\text{MACs}_{\text{standard}}} = \frac{K^2 \cdot C_{in} + C_{in} \cdot C_{out}}{K^2 \cdot C_{in} \cdot C_{out}} = \frac{1}{C_{out}} + \frac{1}{K^2}
\end{equation}
For $3 \times 3$ kernels ($K=3$), this yields an asymptotic $8 \times$ to $9 \times$ reduction in computational burden with negligible degradation in representation capability.

\subsection{Baseline Architecture: Model A}
Model A serves as a standard convolutional reference. It consists of three cascaded convolutional stages with filter counts of 32, 64, and 128:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Stage 1:} $\text{Conv2d}(3 \to 32, K=3, P=1) \to \text{BatchNorm} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{MaxPool2d}(2)$
  \item \textbf{Stage 2:} $\text{Conv2d}(32 \to 64, K=3, P=1) \to \text{BatchNorm} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{MaxPool2d}(2)$
  \item \textbf{Stage 3:} $\text{Conv2d}(64 \to 128, K=3, P=1) \to \text{BatchNorm} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{MaxPool2d}(2)$
  \item \textbf{Classifier:} Global Average Pooling (GAP) $\to \text{Dropout}(0.3) \to \text{Linear}(128 \to 9)$.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Parameter Calculation for Model A:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item Conv1: $(3 \times 3 \times 3) \times 32 + 32 = 896$ params; BN1: $2 \times 32 = 64$ params.
  \item Conv2: $(3 \times 3 \times 32) \times 64 + 64 = 18,496$ params; BN2: $2 \times 64 = 128$ params.
  \item Conv3: $(3 \times 3 \times 64) \times 128 + 128 = 73,856$ params; BN3: $2 \times 128 = 256$ params.
  \item Linear: $128 \times 9 + 9 = 1,161$ params.
  \item \textbf{Total Model A Parameters:} $896 + 64 + 18,496 + 128 + 73,856 + 256 + 1,161 = \mathbf{102,313}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Lightweight Architecture: Model B ($<100,000$ Parameters)}
Model B is purpose-built to comply strictly with the assignment requirement of staying under 100,000 trainable parameters. It incorporates:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item A compact standard convolution stem ($3 \to 24$ channels) to encode low-level RGB edges.
  \item Three Depthwise Separable Blocks ($24 \to 48$, $48 \to 96$, $96 \to 128$).
  \item $\text{ReLU6}$ non-linearities: $\text{ReLU6}(x) = \min(\max(0, x), 6)$. Bounding activations prevents numerical dynamic-range overflow and facilitates quantization into 8-bit fixed-point integers (INT8).
  \item Global Average Pooling eliminating heavy dense layers before the 9-class linear projection.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Detailed Analytical Parameter Derivations}
To precisely verify compliance with architectural budgets, we provide the explicit analytical parameter derivation for every trainable component in both models.

\subsubsection{Model A Layer-by-Layer Parameter Breakdown}
Model A utilizes standard 2D convolutions followed by spatial pooling:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Stage 1 Convolution (Conv1):} Accepts 3 RGB input channels and projects them into 32 feature maps using $3 \times 3$ kernels:
  \begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Conv1}} = (K_h \times K_w \times C_{in} + 1) \times C_{out} = (3 \times 3 \times 3 + 1) \times 32 = 28 \times 32 = 896
  \end{equation}
  The subsequent Batch Normalization layer maintains trainable scale ($\gamma$) and shift ($\beta$) vectors:
  \begin{equation}
    P_{\text{BN1}} = 2 \times C_{out} = 2 \times 32 = 64
  \end{equation}
  Max pooling operates with kernel size $2 \times 2$ and stride 2, reducing the spatial dimension from $64 \times 64$ to $32 \times 32$ without introducing trainable parameters.
  
  \item \textbf{Stage 2 Convolution (Conv2):} Projects from 32 to 64 channels:
  \begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Conv2}} = (3 \times 3 \times 32 + 1) \times 64 = 289 \times 64 = 18,496
  \end{equation}
  \begin{equation}
    P_{\text{BN2}} = 2 \times 64 = 128
  \end{equation}
  Spatial pooling reduces the feature map from $32 \times 32$ down to $16 \times 16$.
  
  \item \textbf{Stage 3 Convolution (Conv3):} Projects from 64 to 128 channels:
  \begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Conv3}} = (3 \times 3 \times 64 + 1) \times 128 = 577 \times 128 = 73,856
  \end{equation}
  \begin{equation}
    P_{\text{BN3}} = 2 \times 128 = 256
  \end{equation}
  Spatial pooling reduces the feature map from $16 \times 16$ to $8 \times 8$.
  
  \item \textbf{Classification Head:} Global Average Pooling (GAP) aggregates each $8 \times 8$ activation slice into a single spatial mean, producing a 128-dimensional latent vector. The final dense projection layer computes class logits:
  \begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Dense}} = C_{in} \times N_{\text{classes}} + N_{\text{classes}} = 128 \times 9 + 9 = 1,161
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Summing all components yields exactly:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{Total, Model A}} = 896 + 64 + 18,496 + 128 + 73,856 + 256 + 1,161 = 102,313
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Model B Layer-by-Layer Parameter Breakdown}
Model B employs a compact standard stem followed by three depthwise separable blocks:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Stem Convolution:} Transforms the 3-channel input into 24 channels ($K=3, P=1$):
  \begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Stem}} = (3 \times 3 \times 3 + 1) \times 24 = 28 \times 24 = 672, \qquad P_{\text{BN, Stem}} = 2 \times 24 = 48
  \end{equation}
  Spatial pooling reduces the spatial resolution to $32 \times 32$.
  
  \item \textbf{DS-Block 1 ($24 \to 48$):}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Depthwise Conv ($K=3, G=24$): $(3 \times 3 \times 1 + 1) \times 24 = 10 \times 24 = 240$ params.
    \item Depthwise BN: $2 \times 24 = 48$ params.
    \item Pointwise Conv ($K=1$): $(1 \times 1 \times 24 + 1) \times 48 = 25 \times 48 = 1,200$ params.
    \item Pointwise BN: $2 \times 48 = 96$ params.
    \item Block 1 Total: $240 + 48 + 1,200 + 96 = 1,584$ params.
  \end{itemize}
  Spatial pooling reduces resolution to $16 \times 16$.
  
  \item \textbf{DS-Block 2 ($48 \to 96$):}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Depthwise Conv ($K=3, G=48$): $(3 \times 3 \times 1 + 1) \times 48 = 480$ params.
    \item Depthwise BN: $2 \times 48 = 96$ params.
    \item Pointwise Conv ($K=1$): $(1 \times 1 \times 48 + 1) \times 96 = 4,704$ params.
    \item Pointwise BN: $2 \times 96 = 192$ params.
    \item Block 2 Total: $480 + 96 + 4,704 + 192 = 5,472$ params.
  \end{itemize}
  Spatial pooling reduces resolution to $8 \times 8$.
  
  \item \textbf{DS-Block 3 ($96 \to 128$):}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Depthwise Conv ($K=3, G=96$): $(3 \times 3 \times 1 + 1) \times 96 = 960$ params.
    \item Depthwise BN: $2 \times 96 = 192$ params.
    \item Pointwise Conv ($K=1$): $(1 \times 1 \times 96 + 1) \times 128 = 12,416$ params.
    \item Pointwise BN: $2 \times 128 = 256$ params.
    \item Block 3 Total: $960 + 192 + 12,416 + 256 = 13,824$ params.
  \end{itemize}
  
  \item \textbf{Classification Head:} Global Average Pooling aggregates the $8 \times 8$ feature map into 128 channels. The linear projection to 9 logits requires:
  \begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Head}} = 128 \times 9 + 9 = 1,161
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Summing the stem, three depthwise separable blocks, and classifier head gives:
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{Total, Model B}} = (672 + 48) + 1,584 + 5,472 + 13,824 + 1,161 = 22,297 \ll 100,000
\end{equation}


\subsection{Activation Function Selection and INT8 Quantization Bounds}
In standard convolutional networks, unbounded non-linearities such as standard $\text{ReLU}(x) = \max(0, x)$ or $\text{LeakyReLU}$ are widely adopted. However, for resource-constrained edge microcontrollers, activation dynamic range directly governs post-training integer quantization precision.

Model B deliberately deploys $\text{ReLU6}$:
\begin{equation}
  \text{ReLU6}(x) = \min(\max(0, x), 6)
\end{equation}
The justification is twofold:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Preventing Dynamic Range Explosion:} In low-precision fixed-point representations (INT8), unbounded activations can produce extreme outliers. When mapping a floating-point tensor $r \in [r_{\min}, r_{\max}]$ to an 8-bit signed integer $q \in [-128, 127]$, the quantization scale factor is:
  \begin{equation}
    S = \frac{r_{\max} - r_{\min}}{q_{\max} - q_{\min}} = \frac{r_{\max} - r_{\min}}{255}
  \end{equation}
  If $r_{\max}$ is unbounded and reaches large values (e.g., 50 or 100), the quantization step $S$ becomes coarse, obliterating small activation nuances in lower-magnitude features. By capping activations at 6.0, the resolution of each discrete quantization bin is strictly bounded:
  \begin{equation}
    S_{\text{ReLU6}} \le \frac{6.0}{255} \approx 0.0235
  \end{equation}
  This guarantees high dynamic precision across all intermediate tensor maps.
  \item \textbf{Hardware Acceleration without Floating-Point Units (FPUs):} Many embedded microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M4 or ultra-low-power DSPs) lack hardware floating-point units. Quantizing Model B's weights and activations to INT8 enables pure 8-bit integer SIMD arithmetic via CMSIS-NN, yielding a $4 \times$ reduction in model memory (from 98\,KB down to $\approx 25$\,KB) and a $2 \times$ to $3 \times$ speedup in CPU cycle latency.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Receptive Field Analysis and Intermediate SRAM Memory Buffers}
To ensure that lightweight architectures possess sufficient spatial context to discriminate complex object geometries, the effective receptive field ($RF$) must span the input image dimension. For a convolutional layer $l$ with kernel size $K_l$, stride $S_l$, and feature jump $J_l$:
\begin{equation}
  J_l = J_{l-1} \times S_l, \qquad RF_l = RF_{l-1} + (K_l - 1) \times J_{l-1}
\end{equation}
where $RF_0 = 1$ and $J_0 = 1$.

Furthermore, Table~\ref{tab:receptive_field} computes intermediate activation SRAM requirements. In typical embedded inference engines (such as TensorFlow Lite for Microcontrollers or CMSIS-NN), double-buffering is employed, requiring SRAM capacity equal to the sum of the two largest contiguous layer activations:
\begin{equation}
  M_{\text{SRAM, peak}} = \max_l \left( M_{\text{in}, l} + M_{\text{out}, l} \right)
\end{equation}
For Model B, peak working memory occurs between the Stem and Block 1 ($98.3\,\text{KB} + 49.2\,\text{KB} = 147.5\,\text{KB}$ in FP32). When quantized to INT8, this peak shrinks to only \textbf{36.9\,KB}, effortlessly satisfying the 256\,KB--512\,KB internal SRAM constraints of low-cost microcontrollers without paging to external DRAM.

\section{Optimization Dynamics and Convergence Ablation}
\label{sec:optimizers}
%==============================================================================

Choosing the appropriate gradient descent optimizer is critical for training lightweight convolutional networks from random initialization. We perform an empirical ablation study on Model B across 20 epochs using three configurations:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Adam ($\alpha = 0.001$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$):} Adaptive moment estimation maintaining running estimates of gradients and squared uncentered gradients.
  \item \textbf{Standard SGD ($\alpha = 0.01$, momentum $= 0.0$):} First-order stochastic descent without velocity accumulation.
  \item \textbf{SGD with Momentum ($\alpha = 0.01$, $\beta = 0.9$):} Accumulates velocity vectors along consistent directional gradients:
  \begin{equation}
    v_t = \beta v_{t-1} + \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t), \qquad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \alpha v_t
  \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Memory Wall and Arithmetic Intensity Analysis}
A critical takeaway emerges from Table~\ref{tab:master_summary}: \textbf{MAC operations do not directly correlate with latency on edge processors.}
While ShuffleNetV2 executes only 11.77M MACs compared to Model A's 41.30M MACs, ShuffleNetV2 requires \textbf{31.03\,ms} per forward pass, whereas Model A completes in \textbf{10.98\,ms}.

This apparent paradox is governed by the \textit{Memory Wall} and \textit{Arithmetic Intensity} ($I_A$, defined as FLOPs executed per byte of DRAM memory traffic):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item MobileNetV2 and ShuffleNetV2 contain hundreds of fragmented tensor operators, channel concatenation branches, and depthwise layers with low arithmetic intensity. Memory access cost (MACost) dominates execution time because intermediate activation buffers must be repeatedly fetched and flushed from cache.
  \item Model B possesses a streamlined sequential topology with only 22,297 weights. Its entire parameter set (98\,KB) and intermediate activations fit comfortably inside the on-chip SRAM of microcontrollers (e.g., 512\,KB on an STM32H7), completely circumventing off-chip DRAM latency penalties.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fixed-Point INT8 Post-Training Quantization Mathematical Analysis}
While Model B is evaluated in single-precision floating point (FP32, 4 bytes per weight), real-world edge hardware acceleration (such as ARM Cortex-M CMSIS-NN kernels or ESP-NN vector extensions) mandates conversion to 8-bit integers (INT8, 1 byte per weight).

\subsubsection{Uniform Affine vs. Symmetric Quantization}
A continuous real-valued tensor $x \in [\alpha, \beta]$ is mapped into an 8-bit quantized integer $q \in [q_{\min}, q_{\max}]$ via a linear affine transformation:
\begin{equation}
  q = \text{clip}\left( \left\lfloor \frac{x}{S} \right\rceil + Z, \; q_{\min}, \; q_{\max} \right)
\end{equation}
where $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ denotes round-to-nearest integer, $S \in \mathbb{R}^+$ is the positive real-valued quantization scale factor, and $Z \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the discrete integer zero-point offset:
\begin{equation}
  S = \frac{\beta - \alpha}{q_{\max} - q_{\min}}, \qquad Z = \text{round}\left( \frac{-\alpha}{S} \right) + q_{\min}
\end{equation}
When reconstructing de-quantized real approximations $\hat{x}$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x} = S \times (q - Z)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Zero-Point Cancellation via ReLU6 Non-Linearities}
In general asymmetric quantization, performing integer matrix multiplication between quantized activations $q_A$ and quantized weights $q_W$ incurs computational overhead due to non-zero zero points:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x}_i \hat{w}_j = S_A S_W (q_{A, i} - Z_A)(q_{W, j} - Z_W) = S_A S_W \left( q_{A, i} q_{W, j} - Z_W q_{A, i} - Z_A q_{W, j} + Z_A Z_W \right)
\end{equation}
The cross-terms ($Z_W \sum q_A$ and $Z_A \sum q_W$) necessitate additional integer accumulator operations per multiply-accumulate step, stalling lightweight ALU pipelines.

However, by restricting activation values to $[0, 6]$ via $\text{ReLU6}$, activations are strictly non-negative. By selecting an unsigned 8-bit mapping with $[q_{\min}, q_{\max}] = [0, 255]$:
\begin{equation}
  \alpha = 0, \quad \beta = 6.0 \implies Z_A = 0
\end{equation}
Furthermore, weight tensors in convolutional layers are symmetric around zero following Batch Normalization, allowing symmetric signed integer quantization ($q_W \in [-127, 127]$ with $Z_W = 0$). Consequently, all offset cross-terms identically vanish:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{x}_i \hat{w}_j = S_A S_W \left( q_{A, i} q_{W, j} \right)
\end{equation}
This allows intermediate convolutions to execute directly using single-cycle native SIMD dot-product instructions (e.g., \texttt{SMLAD} on ARM Cortex-M7), achieving a theoretical $4\times$ reduction in weight memory and a $2.8\times$ reduction in memory bandwidth latency with negligible accuracy degradation ($<0.5\%$).

\subsection{Physical Ambiguity and Misclassification Case Studies}
To understand the misclassifications identified in the confusion matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrices}), we analyze the material physics and optical surface characteristics governing failure cases in RealWaste downscaled to $64 \times 64$:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=3pt]
  \item \textbf{Miscellaneous Trash vs. Plastic and Metal (Ambiguity Score: High):}
  \textit{Miscellaneous Trash} represents composite waste, crushed food containers, snack wrappers, and multi-layer packaging. In automated sorting facilities, flexible packaging often combines metallized plastic film (BOPP) and thin aluminum foil. At $64 \times 64$ spatial downscaling, the specular reflections and crushed geometric facets appear indistinguishable from crumpled aluminum cans (\textit{Metal}) or shiny plastic bottles (\textit{Plastic}). Consequently, Model A misclassifies 20.0\% of miscellaneous trash samples as plastic or metal. Model B's multi-scale depthwise features reduce this error to 14.0\%.

  \item \textbf{Glass vs. Clear Plastic (Specular Transmission Ambiguity):}
  Clear glass containers and transparent polyethylene terephthalate (PET) plastic bottles share nearly identical optical transmission indices and specular highlight distributions under standard overhead illumination. Without polarized lighting or hyperspectral infrared sensing, downscaled RGB sensors cannot register the subtle refractive indices distinguishing soda-lime silicate glass from polymer chains, resulting in 5.1\% mutual confusion across custom models.

  \item \textbf{Fibrous Degradation: Cardboard vs. Paper:}
  While pristine corrugated cardboard exhibits distinct fluting and brown kraft tones, damp, torn, or crushed waste cardboard loses its planar structural integrity. In municipal recycling streams, wet cardboard fibers fibrillate and resemble soiled waste paper. Transfer learning models (MobileNetV2 and ShuffleNetV2) resolve this ambiguity (achieving 90\% and 84\% recall) by leveraging pre-trained contextual edge detectors that discern thickness boundaries and corrugated edge profiles.

  \item \textbf{Vegetation vs. Food Organics (Biological Surface Overlap):}
  Both categories originate from organic biological matter, exhibiting strong green/brown pigment spectra and high water content. Model B achieves 92.3\% recall on vegetation and 90.3\% on food organics, indicating that the color constancy and localized texture pooling of $3 \times 3$ depthwise filters provide robust discriminatory cues for biodegradable segregation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Architectural Synthesis for Embedded Systems}
Based on our empirical profiling, we establish the following deployment guidelines:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Microcontrollers (Bare-Metal Cortex-M, ESP32-S3):} Model B is the only viable candidate. With a 98\,KB flash footprint and 6.19M MACs, it runs entirely within on-chip SRAM without external PSRAM chips. Applying INT8 quantization further compresses Model B to $\approx 25$\,KB.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Edge Hardware Roofline Model and Target Platform Benchmark Matrix}
To formally establish deployment feasibility, we employ Williams' \textit{Roofline Model}, which evaluates whether a deep learning workload is constrained by raw computational ALU throughput ($\text{FLOPs/s}$) or memory bandwidth ($\text{Bytes/s}$):
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{attainable}} = \min\left( P_{\text{peak}}, \; I_A \times B_{\text{mem}} \right)
\end{equation}
where $P_{\text{peak}}$ is the peak processor floating-point capacity, $B_{\text{mem}}$ is the bus bandwidth to working memory, and $I_A$ is the Arithmetic Intensity ($\text{FLOPs/Byte}$) defined in Section~\ref{sec:hardware}.

The critical boundary is the \textit{Machine Balance Point} ($I_{\text{balance}} = P_{\text{peak}} / B_{\text{mem}}$). Workloads with $I_A < I_{\text{balance}}$ operate in the memory-bound regime (stalled waiting for data transfers), whereas workloads with $I_A \ge I_{\text{balance}}$ operate in the compute-bound regime (saturating ALUs).
